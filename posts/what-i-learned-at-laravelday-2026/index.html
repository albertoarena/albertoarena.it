What I learned at LaravelDay 2026

I spent a day at LaravelDay 2026 in Verona. Here is what I learned from the talks, why I think going to conferences is worth it, and why PHP is still alive.

November 19, 2026 6 min read

Arriving in Verona

I work 100% remote. Most days, the people I talk to about Laravel are names on a screen. I love working remotely, and I’ve been doing it since April 2015, but sometimes I miss real interaction.

So I decided to go to LaravelDay 2026 in person. It was held on Wednesday 18/11 in Verona. GrUSP organised it, and all the talks were in Italian.

It was a long journey from Sicily, and I had to change my flight for external reasons and take one early on the morning of the conference, but I was happy to be there.

What I learned

Write it, find it, check it

Roberto Gallea gave a nice talk about making a Laravel app readable for AI. He made a good comparison, saying that many Laravel applications are “telepathic”: basically, a human developer needs to know how they are written, and if onboarding is challenging for a new colleague, it is even harder for an AI agent.

One suggestion of his:

Rule Explanation
Write it. The rules are written down, not guessed.
Find it. The rules are easy to find.
Check it. The code can be checked against them.

I want to review some of my older projects and see if they are readable for AI.

A year of hexagonal architecture

Daniele Barbaro described a year with hexagonal architecture, and he was honest about what did not work. The good part of the work is now an open-source package. He shared everything from the talk on one page: slides, example repo, the prompt he gave the agent, and his article A year of hexagonal architecture in Laravel.

Hexagonal architecture keeps the business logic in the middle and connects the framework, the database and other outside services to it through interfaces.

I had already walked part of this road through DDD, for example in my package albertoarena/laravel-event-sourcing-generator. But my way was different from what Daniele suggested, because in my domain-driven approach I keep everything about a domain in the same folder: actions, events, models, projectors, reactors and so on.

He suggested a different folder structure, with three layers:

app/
├── Domain/
│     └── ...
├── Application/
│     └── ...
└── Infrastructure/
      └── ...

I want to try his package plin-code/laravel-clean-architecture in a project of mine:

composer require plin-code/laravel-clean-architecture
php artisan clean-arch:install --migrate-app   # moves existing code: use a fresh or test project
composer dump-autoload

Open source from one developer’s need

Andrea Pollastri began Cipi in 2018 as a tool for his own deploys. Today it is open source, with over 1,100 stars on GitHub.

His talk encouraged me. I told him so the same evening, over a beer. I have been working a lot on Laravel Truss, an ER diagram (ERD) tool for Laravel, and sometimes I wondered if the effort was worth it. Andrea’s talk was an inspiration for any open-source contributor: do not be ashamed to publish, accept good criticism and comments, ignore haters, and go ahead.

I love this line of his:

A public vulnerability is nothing to be ashamed of. It’s the process working.

By chance, during the conference week Truss passed 80,000 installs, so that was a nice personal reward for my perseverance.

His talk is one more reason to keep investing in open source.

The rest of the day

  • Filippo Donadoni showed that a DB_URL in .env can make tests run on staging. What I liked is the process he used to find a subtle bug, which he reported as an issue and which was later fixed in Laravel.
  • Alberto Peripolli presented Laravel Database MCP 1.0, which lets an AI read a database in read-only mode, without raw SQL. I shared it on X and LinkedIn, and it got good interest.
  • Giacomo Fabbian moved from one branch per client to one codebase, with a flag per tenant on each new feature. His talk highlighted how hard it can be to maintain an application used by several clients. Food for thought.
  • Leonardo Rossi went through Pulse, Nightwatch and OpenTelemetry for observability. Wow, there’s a lot I can improve with telemetry!

Why being there matters

I was already in touch with many people online, but a network is built when you spend some time chatting.

I met in person people I knew only online: Roberto Gallea, Alberto Peripolli, Giulio Pecorella, Erik and Jenno. I also talked with most of the speakers.

Giulio Pecorella told me he reads my articles, and he wrote a kind LinkedIn comment about them. That was a welcome recognition for my little contribution to the Laravel community.

“PHP is dead”, said a room full of PHP developers

Giulio Pecorella gave a very funny lightning talk with the proof that PHP is dead. He gave it to a room of PHP developers.

I believe that PHP is vibrant and alive, and I curate phplives.dev where I share real things people shipped with PHP in the last 6 months.

The closing panel asked a question that stayed with me: with AI around, should you contribute to a project, copy it, or write your own? One line from it: “Opening a PR takes seconds, reviewing it doesn’t.”

What to do with this

  • Check phpunit.xml in your apps. If .env sets DB_URL, tests can run against that database. New Laravel apps have had <env name="DB_URL" value=""/> since 26/02/2026, older ones do not. See laravel/framework #58985.
  • Look at Cipi if you want to own your deploys.
  • Look at Laravel Database MCP.
  • Try Laravel Clean Architecture on a test project. Daniele asks for feedback, especially about what did not work.
  • Go to a conference near you. LaravelDay is one, and PHP conferences exist in many countries.

Thank you to GrUSP and to all the speakers. It was nice to meet everyone, and I look forward to being at LaravelDay again next year!
